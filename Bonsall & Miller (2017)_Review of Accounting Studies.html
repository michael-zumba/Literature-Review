1. Literature Review Components¶

  • Title: The Impact of Narrative Disclosure Readability on Bond Ratings and the Cost of Debt
  • Journal: Review of Accounting Studies
  • Authors: Samuel B. Bonsall IV, Brian P. Miller
  • In-text Citation Format: Bonsall & Miller (2017)
  • Overview: This study examines how the readability of financial disclosures affects bond ratings, rating agency disagreement, and the cost of debt. The authors use the 1998 Plain English Mandate as a quasi-experiment to improve causal identification.
  • Key Contribution to Field: Provides empirical evidence that less readable disclosures lead to less favorable bond ratings, greater rating agency disagreement, and higher costs of debt.
  • Policy/Practical Implications: Supports regulatory efforts to improve disclosure readability, as clearer disclosures can reduce information asymmetry and lower firms' cost of capital.
  • Methodology:
    • Uses panel data of 3,659 corporate bond issuances (1994–2014).
    • Primary readability measure: Bog Index (BOG).
    • Difference-in-differences (DID) design exploiting the 1998 Plain English Mandate.
    • Controls for firm complexity, financial ratios, and other textual attributes (e.g., tone, uncertainty).
  • Results:
    • Less readable disclosures are associated with worse bond ratings (higher default risk).
    • Lower readability increases rating agency disagreement (split ratings and larger gaps).
    • Higher readability reduces the cost of debt (77 basis points narrower spreads for treated firms).
  • Discussion: Results are robust to alternative readability measures (e.g., Fog Index, file size). The DID analysis suggests causality, as readability improvements driven by the mandate led to better bond market outcomes.
  • Research Gaps/Areas for Improvement:
    • Limited to U.S. firms; international evidence is needed.
    • Does not fully disentangle whether readability effects stem from managerial obfuscation or inherent complexity.
  • Paper's Strengths and Weaknesses:
    • Strengths: Strong identification strategy (DID), multifaceted readability measures, comprehensive controls.
    • Weaknesses: Relies on 10-K filings; other disclosure channels (e.g., earnings calls) are not examined.
  • Limitations and Future Research:
    • Suggests examining emerging markets and non-textual disclosure channels.
    • Calls for research on how readability interacts with other disclosure attributes (e.g., visuals, hyperlinks).

2. Variables Summary¶

  • Dependent Variables:
    • Bond Ratings (M_RATING, S_RATING): Numeric mappings (1 = AAA/Aaa, 21 = C/C). Data source: Moody’s/S&P.
    • Rating Disagreement (SPLIT, SPLIT_GAP): Binary (1 if Moody’s ≠ S&P) and absolute rating difference. Data source: Mergent FISD.
    • Cost of Debt (CREDIT_SPREAD): Offering yield minus Treasury yield. Data source: Mergent FISD.
  • Independent Variable:
    • Readability (BOG): Plain English score (higher = worse readability). Data source: StyleWriter software.
  • Control Variables:
    • Firm size, leverage, profitability (ROA), earnings volatility, analyst following, and macroeconomic uncertainty (VIX).
    • Textual attributes: Tone, uncertainty, forward-looking statements, risk disclosures.

3. Methodology Summary¶

  • Analytical Methods:
    • Ordered logistic regression (bond ratings).
    • Binary/ordered logistic regression (rating disagreement).
    • OLS (cost of debt).
  • Model Equations:
    • Ratings: Ordered logit with firm/year fixed effects.
    • Credit spread: OLS with controls for rating levels and disagreement.
  • Identification Strategy: DID using the 1998 Plain English Mandate (non-shelf registrants = treated, shelf registrants = control).
  • Robustness Tests: Alternative readability measures (Fog Index, file size), placebo tests.
  • Data Sampling: 3,659 bond issuances (1994–2014), excluding financials/utilities and winsorizing outliers.

4. Theory Summary¶

  • Name of Theory: Agency Theory
  • Origin: Jensen & Meckling (1976)
  • Description: Managers may obfuscate disclosures to hide poor performance, increasing information asymmetry.
  • Application in Study: Less readable disclosures amplify uncertainty, leading to higher perceived default risk and rating disagreement.
  • Empirical Validation: Supported by negative association between readability and bond ratings.
  • Competing Theories: Stewardship theory (not supported).

5. Data Source Summary¶

  • Primary Data Sources:
    • Bond ratings: Mergent FISD.
    • Readability: 10-K filings processed via StyleWriter.
    • Financial controls: Compustat, CRSP.
  • Temporal Coverage: 1994–2014.
  • Sample Size: 3,659 bond issuances (1,097 firms).
  • Data Cleaning: Winsorized at 1%/99%, excluded filings with <3,000 words.

6. Key Empirical Findings¶

  • Economic Significance:
    • A move from the 75th to 50th percentile in BOG saves $440,000 annually per $430 million issuance.
  • Cross-study Comparison: Consistent with prior equity market studies (e.g., Li 2008) but extends to debt markets.
