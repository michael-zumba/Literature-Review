1. Literature Review Components¶

  • Title: Management earnings forecasts and other forward-looking statements
  • Journal: Journal of Accounting and Economics
  • Authors: Zahn Bozanic, Darren T. Roulstone, Andrew Van Buskirk
  • In-text Citation Format: Bozanic et al. (2018)
  • Overview: The paper examines forward-looking statements (FLS) in earnings announcements, distinguishing between quantitative earnings forecasts ("forecast-like" FLS) and other qualitative/non-earnings FLS. It investigates their market impact, determinants, and how uncertainty influences disclosure choices.
  • Key Contribution to Field:
    • Demonstrates that non-forecast-like FLS (qualitative/non-earnings) elicit significant investor and analyst responses, challenging the focus on quantitative earnings forecasts in prior literature.
    • Shows that managers issue more non-forecast-like FLS (but fewer forecast-like FLS) when uncertainty is high, suggesting substitution effects in disclosure strategies.
    • Identifies that managers prioritize altering the content (earnings vs. non-earnings) over the form (quantitative vs. qualitative) of FLS under uncertainty.
  • Policy/Practical Implications:
    • Regulators and investors should consider qualitative FLS as meaningful disclosures, not just quantitative forecasts.
    • Firms may use non-earnings FLS to manage uncertainty while avoiding the costs of missed earnings projections.
  • Methodology:
    • Textual analysis of 59,327 earnings announcements (2004–2014) to classify FLS along two dimensions: (1) earnings/non-earnings and (2) quantitative/non-quantitative.
    • Validates classification using I/B/E/S earnings forecasts.
    • Tests market reactions (absolute returns, analyst forecast revisions) and determinants (uncertainty proxies like earnings volatility, analyst dispersion).
  • Results:
    • Non-forecast-like FLS generate significant market reactions, even when issued without earnings forecasts.
    • Uncertainty reduces forecast-like FLS but increases other FLS.
    • Earnings-related FLS are more sensitive to uncertainty than quantitative FLS.
  • Discussion:
    • Highlights limitations of using earnings forecasts as a proxy for overall voluntary disclosure.
    • Suggests that non-earnings FLS help managers mitigate litigation and reputational risks under uncertainty.
  • Research Gaps/Areas for Improvement:
    • Limited to earnings announcements; future work could examine 10-K/10-Q filings.
    • Does not fully categorize types of non-earnings FLS (e.g., revenue vs. strategic plans).
  • Paper's Strengths and Weaknesses:
    • Strengths: Novel textual analysis, large sample, clear distinction between FLS types.
    • Weaknesses: Reliance on keyword-based classification (potential misclassification), US-centric sample.
  • Limitations and Future Research:
    • Explore cross-country differences in FLS usage.
    • Investigate how FLS interact with other disclosure channels (e.g., conference calls).

2. Variables Summary¶

  • Dependent Variables:
    • Investor Response: Absolute 3-day earnings announcement returns.
    • Analyst Response: Change in accuracy of next-quarter earnings forecasts.
    • FLS Issuance: Proportion of earnings announcements containing forecast-like or other FLS.
  • Independent Variables:
    • Uncertainty Proxies: Loss indicators, earnings volatility, analyst dispersion, implied volatility.
    • FLS Types: Quantitative/earnings-related vs. non-quantitative/non-earnings.
  • Control Variables: Firm size, book-to-market, lagged returns, analyst following, earnings surprises.

3. Methodology Summary¶

  • Analytical Methods: OLS regressions, probit models, seemingly unrelated regressions (SUR).
  • Identification Strategy: Uses lagged FLS measures and firm/year fixed effects to address endogeneity.
  • Robustness Tests:
    • Validates FLS classification against I/B/E/S forecasts.
    • Excludes "borderline" FLS (e.g., quantitative non-earnings metrics like revenue).
  • Data Sampling: 59,327 earnings announcements (2004–2014) from S&P 500 firms with analyst coverage.
  • Software/Tools: Perl for textual analysis, Stata for regressions.

4. Theory Summary¶

  • Name of Theory: Agency Theory, Disclosure Theory.
  • Origin: Jensen & Meckling (1976), Verrecchia (2001).
  • Description: Managers balance the benefits of disclosure (reducing information asymmetry) against costs (litigation, reputation).
  • Application in Study: Explains why managers substitute non-earnings FLS for earnings forecasts under uncertainty.
  • Competing Theories: Stewardship theory (not directly tested).

5. Data Source Summary¶

  • Primary Sources: SEC EDGAR (8-K filings), I/B/E/S (earnings forecasts), CRSP, Compustat.
  • Temporal Coverage: 2004–2014.
  • Sample Size: 59,327 firm-quarters.
  • Data Cleaning: Removed boilerplate text, winsorized variables.

6. Key Empirical Findings¶

  • Economic Significance: A 1 SD increase in non-forecast-like FLS has a market reaction comparable to a 1 SD increase in forecast-like FLS.
  • Cross-study Comparison: Contrasts with Hutton et al. (2003), which found supplemental disclosures only matter with earnings forecasts.

7. Replication Materials¶
