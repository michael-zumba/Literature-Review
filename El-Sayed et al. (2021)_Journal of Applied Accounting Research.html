1. Literature Review Components¶

  • Title: The influence of narrative disclosure readability, information ordering and graphical representations on non-professional investors’ judgment: evidence from an emerging market
  • Journal: Journal of Applied Accounting Research
  • Authors: Dalia Hussein El-Sayed, Eman Adel, Omar Elmougy, Nadeen Fawzy, Nada Hatem, Farida Elhakey
  • In-text Citation Format: El-Sayed et al. (2021)
  • Overview: Examines how manipulation in corporate narrative disclosures (readability, information order) and graphical representations bias non-professional investors’ judgments in Egypt.
  • Key Contribution to Field: First study to test the combined effect of readability, information order, and graphs on investor judgments in an emerging market (Egypt). Extends the Belief Adjustment Model (BAM) and agency theory.
  • Policy/Practical Implications: Highlights the need for regulatory guidelines on narrative/graphical disclosures to mitigate investor bias. Encourages investors to use multiple information sources.
  • Methodology: Three 2×2 between-subjects experiments with accounting/finance students as non-professional investor proxies.
  • Results:
    • Simple (readable) disclosures improve judgment accuracy; complex disclosures induce recency effects.
    • Graphs moderate recency effects in complex disclosures but not primacy effects in simple ones.
  • Discussion: Supports BAM’s recency effect predictions for complex disclosures but not primacy effects for simple ones. Aligns with processing fluency theory.
  • Research Gaps/Areas for Improvement: Limited to Egyptian context; small sample size; excludes other impression management strategies (e.g., tone management).
  • Paper's Strengths and Weaknesses:
    • Strengths: Novel multi-strategy experimental design; emerging market focus.
    • Weaknesses: Generalizability constraints (student proxies, single-country sample).
  • Limitations and Future Research: Suggests testing with real investors, longer information sets, and other impression management tools (e.g., thematic manipulation).

2. Variables Summary¶

  • Dependent Variable(s):
    • Investors’ judgment (firm’s future performance)
    • Measurement: 6-point Likert scale (4 questions, e.g., "Company’s future is bright").
    • Economic Significance: Higher scores indicate more positive judgments.
    • Data Source: Experimental responses.
  • Independent Variable(s):
    • Readability (Simple/Complex)
    • Measurement: SEC Plain English Handbook criteria; Gunning Fog Index (Simple: ~10.5; Complex: ~15.3).
    • Information Order (Good-Bad News Order)
    • Graphical Representation (Presence/Absence of Positive Graph)
  • Control Variables:
    • Participant demographics (age, gender, investment experience).
    • Justification: Ensures homogeneity across experimental groups.

3. Methodology Summary¶

  • Analytical Methods: Mann-Whitney U tests for between-group comparisons.
  • Model Equations: Non-parametric tests due to ordinal Likert-scale data.
  • Identification Strategy: Random assignment to experimental conditions.
  • Robustness Tests: Cronbach’s alpha (α=0.829) confirmed scale reliability.
  • Data Sampling:
    • Criteria: Final-year accounting/finance students (proxy for non-professional investors).
    • Time Period: February 2020 (pre-COVID-19).
    • Sample Size: 168 participants (56/52/60 across three experiments).
  • Software/Tools Used: Likely SPSS/Stata (not explicitly stated).

4. Theory Summary¶

  • Name of Theory: Belief Adjustment Model (BAM), Agency Theory
  • Origin of Theory: Hogarth & Einhorn (1992); Jensen & Meckling (1976)
  • Description of Theory:
    • BAM: Predicts primacy/recency effects in belief updating based on information order/complexity.
    • Agency Theory: Managers may manipulate disclosures to exploit information asymmetry.
  • Application in Study: Tests BAM’s order-effect predictions; links impression management to agency costs.
  • Empirical Validation: Recency effects confirmed for complex disclosures; no primacy effects for simple ones.
  • Competing Theories Considered: Processing fluency theory (Rennekamp, 2012).

5. Data Source Summary¶

  • Dependent Variables: Investor judgments (experimental responses).
  • Independent Variables:
    • Readability/order/graphs (researcher-constructed stimuli based on real Egyptian firm data).
  • Temporal Coverage: Single-time experiments (February 2020).
  • Sample Selection Criteria: Egyptian accounting/finance students; no prior exposure to stimuli.
  • Data Cleaning Procedures: No missing responses; winsorization not needed.
  • Data Limitations: Student proxies may lack real-world investment experience.

6. Key Empirical Findings¶

  • Statistical Significance:
    • Recency effect in complex disclosures (p=0.035).
    • Graph moderation effect (p=0.027).
  • Economic Significance:
    • 1 SD increase in readability improved judgments by ~0.5 points (Likert scale).
  • Cross-study Comparison: Consistent with Dilla et al. (2013) on graph effects; contradicts Hellmann et al. (2017) on graph moderation.

8. Discipline-Specific Enhancements¶

  • GAAP/IFRS Standards: Egyptian accounting standards referenced (lack of narrative disclosure rules).
  • Corporate Governance Mechanism: Highlights weak regulatory oversight in Egypt.
  • Financial Regulation Context: Advocates for stricter disclosure guidelines.

Hypothesis Development:
- H1a/H1b: Supported (readability affects judgments; p<0.05).
- H2a: Not supported (no primacy effect; p=0.877).
- H2b/H3b: Supported (recency/graph effects; p<0.05).
- H3a: Not supported (no graph moderation in simple disclosures; p=0.504).
