1. Literature Review Components¶

  • Title: Managerial Extraversion and Corporate Voluntary Disclosure
  • Journal: Contemporary Accounting Research
  • Authors: Florian Eugster, Jenni Kallunki, Juha-Pekka Kallunki, Henrik Nilsson
  • In-text Citation Format: Eugster et al. (2024)
  • Overview:
  • Examines how CEOs' and CFOs' personality trait of extraversion (measured via psychological tests) influences corporate voluntary disclosure.

  • Uses Swedish military enlistment data (extraversion scores) and firm-level disclosure rankings (1999–2015).

  • Key Contribution to Field:

  • Provides novel empirical evidence linking managerial extraversion to higher voluntary disclosure.
  • Addresses endogeneity concerns by using pre-career psychological test scores (unaffected by firm-specific factors).

  • Expands on prior studies (e.g., Liao et al., 2023) by using a comprehensive disclosure score (financial/non-financial) and longitudinal data.

  • Policy/Practical Implications:

  • Suggests that personality assessments of executives could inform stakeholder expectations about disclosure practices.

  • Highlights the role of managerial discretion and job demands in amplifying extraversion effects.

  • Methodology:

  • Data: 1,905 firm-years, 225 Swedish firms, 578 CEOs/CFOs.
  • Variables:
    • Dependent: Voluntary disclosure scores (total/annual/quarterly/website).
    • Independent: Extraversion (1–5 scale from military tests).
    • Controls: Managerial (IQ, tenure, education) and firm-level (size, ROA, leverage) factors.

  • Models: OLS regressions with firm/year fixed effects, robustness tests (e.g., subsamples, endogeneity checks).

  • Results:

  • Positive association between extraversion and voluntary disclosure (e.g., 1 SD ↑ in extraversion → 9.4% ↑ in disclosure score).
  • Effects stronger for CEOs (vs. CFOs) and under high discretion (long tenure, concentrated industries) or high job demands (IFRS adoption).

  • Extraverted managers speak more, provide more forward-looking disclosures, and use more optimistic tones in earnings calls.

  • Discussion:

  • Supports upper echelons theory: Managers’ traits shape firm outcomes.

  • Extraversion’s stability (from age 18) suggests long-term impact on disclosure policies.

  • Research Gaps/Areas for Improvement:

  • Limited to Swedish male executives (generalizability concerns).

  • Future research could explore female managers or cross-country comparisons.

  • Paper’s Strengths and Weaknesses:

  • Strengths: Unique extraversion measure, robust controls, multi-channel disclosure analysis.

  • Weaknesses: Reliance on military data (may not capture post-adolescent personality changes).

  • Limitations and Future Research:

  • Replicate in other jurisdictions with diverse gender representation.
  • Investigate interactions between extraversion and other traits (e.g., narcissism).

2. Variables Summary¶

  • Dependent Variables:
  • DISC_T (Total Disclosure Score): Scaled sum of annual/quarterly/website disclosure rankings.
    • Source: Swedish Stockholders’ Association (SSA) and Kanton.

  • DISC_A/DISC_Q/DISC_W: Sub-scores for annual/quarterly/website disclosures.

  • Independent Variable:

  • EXTRAVERSION: CEO/CFO extraversion (1–5 scale from military psychologist assessments).

    • Source: Swedish Military Forces.

  • Control Variables:

  • Managerial: IQ, height, education (business/law degrees), tenure, age.
  • Firm-Level: Size, leverage, ROA, analyst coverage, IFRS adoption.
    • Sources: Compustat, I/B/E/S, Thomson Reuters.

3. Methodology Summary¶

  • Analytical Method: Panel OLS with firm/year fixed effects.
  • Model Equation:
    [ \text{DISCLOSURE}{jt} = \alpha + \beta_1 \text{EXTRAVERSION}_i + \gamma X + \text{Firm/Year FE} + \epsilon ]
  • Identification Strategy:
  • Extraversion scores pre-date corporate roles (mitigates reverse causality).
  • Firm FE controls for time-invariant heterogeneity.
  • Robustness Tests:
  • Excluding low-extraversion managers.
  • Partitioned samples (e.g., high vs. low discretion).
  • Software/Tools: Stata (standard errors clustered by firm).

4. Theory Summary¶

  • Name of Theory: Upper Echelons Theory (Hambrick & Mason, 1984).
  • Origin: Strategic management literature.
  • Description: Top executives’ backgrounds/traits influence organizational outcomes.
  • Application: Extraverted managers → higher disclosure due to sociability/dominance traits.
  • Competing Theories: Stewardship theory (not supported; extraversion effects persist).

5. Data Source Summary¶

  • Temporal Coverage: 1999–2015.
  • Sample Selection: Swedish listed firms with CEO/CFO changes.
  • Data Cleaning: Winsorized at 1%/99%, extrapolated missing 2006 data.
  • Limitations: Male-only sample (military conscription bias).

6. Key Empirical Findings¶

  • Statistical Significance: Extraversion coefficients significant at 1–5% levels.
  • Economic Significance: 1 SD ↑ in extraversion → 0.014 ↑ in DISC_T (~9.4% of SD).
  • Cross-study Comparison: Consistent with Liao et al. (2023) but uses exogenous trait measures.

7. Replication Materials¶

  • Data Availability: Military data restricted; firm-level data from SSA/Compustat.
  • Code Repository: Not mentioned.

8. Discipline-Specific Enhancements¶

  • GAAP/IFRS: Controls for IFRS adoption effects.
  • Corporate Governance: Links extraversion to disclosure under high discretion.

Hypothesis Development¶

  • H1: Extraversion → + disclosure (Supported).
  • H2: High discretion strengthens H1 (Supported).
  • H3: High job demands strengthen H1 (Partially supported).
